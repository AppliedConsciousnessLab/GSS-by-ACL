## Overview

The JANICE Safety Metrics framework defines measurable indicators used to evaluate the effectiveness of child protection systems.

While ethical principles and operational frameworks guide the design of safety systems, measurable outcomes are necessary to determine whether those systems are functioning effectively.

Metrics allow researchers, policymakers, and institutions to assess progress, identify weaknesses, and improve protective environments for children.

## Measurement Philosophy

Child safety systems should be evaluated using transparent, evidence-based metrics.

Metrics must focus on improving protective environments rather than creating bureaucratic targets that incentivize underreporting.

The goal is not to minimize reported cases, but to maximize the system’s ability to detect, prevent, and respond to harm.

Effective metrics therefore measure:

• prevention effectiveness  
• detection accessibility  
• response timeliness  
• survivor recovery support  
• institutional accountability

---

## Prevention Metrics

Prevention metrics evaluate structural conditions that influence child safety.

Examples include:

• child poverty rates  
• housing stability indicators  
• school attendance rates  
• access to childcare services  
• availability of youth development programs

Research consistently demonstrates that improving these structural indicators reduces vulnerability to exploitation.

---

## Detection Metrics

Detection metrics measure the accessibility and responsiveness of reporting systems.

Examples include:

• time from report submission to initial review  
• percentage of reports investigated  
• number of accessible reporting channels  
• public awareness of reporting mechanisms

Healthy detection systems encourage reporting rather than discouraging it.

Increases in reporting may indicate improved system trust rather than increased harm.

---

## Response Metrics

Response metrics evaluate how quickly and effectively institutions respond when harm is suspected.

Examples include:

• response time from report to protective action  
• time required to initiate investigation  
• access to emergency child protection services  
• multidisciplinary coordination efficiency

Rapid and coordinated response reduces the duration and severity of harm.

---

## Survivor Support Metrics

Long-term recovery requires access to support services.

Metrics may include:

• access to trauma-informed therapy  
• educational continuity support  
• stable housing for affected families  
• long-term follow-up care availability

These indicators measure whether survivors receive adequate support during recovery.

---

## Institutional Accountability Metrics

Institutions responsible for child welfare must maintain transparent accountability systems.

Indicators may include:

• compliance with reporting requirements  
• disciplinary action against confirmed perpetrators  
• transparency of investigation outcomes  
• independent oversight participation

Accountability metrics discourage institutional secrecy and encourage ethical governance.

---

## Digital Safety Metrics

As children increasingly interact online, digital safety indicators are essential.

Examples include:

• platform response time for harmful content reports  
• detection rates for grooming patterns  
• removal time for exploitative material  
• transparency of moderation policies

These metrics evaluate whether digital environments prioritize child protection.

---

## Long-Term Societal Indicators

Child safety systems influence broader societal outcomes.

Relevant long-term indicators include:

• mental health outcomes among youth populations  
• community trust in institutions  
• reductions in human trafficking cases  
• improved educational attainment

These indicators reflect the broader impact of effective child protection systems.
